How to Choose a Character Worth Guessing

By Kent B Williams · Published 27 November 2026 · About the writer

Every round begins with a decision that most players make in two seconds and rarely think about again: which character to hold in their head. That choice determines how long the round runs, how interesting it feels, and whether the final guess lands. Choosing well is the part of the game the player actually controls.

What the engine needs from a character

The engine works from traits, not from names. Each character in the roster has a recorded answer for a fixed set of broad questions: realistic or fictional, human or not, powered or not, masked or not, employed or not, and so on. A character who can be described clearly with traits like those is easy to work with. A character whose defining feature is a personality, a relationship or a running joke is much harder, because none of those things can be asked about directly.

So the first test of a good pick is simple: could you answer twenty yes-or-no questions about this character out loud, without hesitating? If the answer is no, the round will probably go badly, and it will not be the engine's fault.

Picks that make a short round

Characters with one strong, unusual combination of traits are guessed quickly. A hero who is also a billionaire. A queen with ice magic. A plumber who is not human in any meaningful way. The engine finds these fast because the combination is rare in the roster, and a single answer can eliminate most of the field.

Short rounds are not failures. They are the game working as intended. If you want the pleasure of watching the questions arrive in an order that feels inevitable, pick a distinctive character and answer with confidence.

Picks that make a long round

Characters whose traits overlap with someone else make long rounds. Two soldiers without powers. Two detectives with no equipment. Two anti-heroes who are neither clearly good nor clearly villainous. When several characters share most of their answers, the engine has to ask narrower and narrower questions, and each one removes fewer candidates.

These are the interesting rounds, and they are also the fragile ones. A vague answer in a long round does more damage than a vague answer in a short one, because there is less room left to recover. If you want a long round, commit to answering carefully.

The version problem

Most characters in the roster have more than one version. A comic hero has a dozen film appearances. A detective has a century of adaptations. A television character changes between seasons. The engine cannot know which version you mean, so it asks questions that assume one consistent version, and the round breaks if your answers drift between several.

The fix is unglamorous: decide on one version before the first question and answer only for it. If a question does not fit that version, use a soft answer rather than switching to another version to make it fit.

Choosing by category

Film characters are the safest first pick. They usually have one defining look and one clear role, which gives the costume and role questions something to work with. Television characters are a step harder because a long series changes them. Anime and game characters reward specific knowledge: species, powers and equipment carry a lot of weight, and a player who knows the series can answer precisely.

Historical figures are their own case. Every one of them answers no to the fictional question, which removes a huge chunk of the roster immediately. What remains is a matter of era, role and reputation, and reputation is where answers get soft.

Making a round harder without making it unfair

If the game feels too easy, the answer is not to lie about your character. A lie makes the round random rather than difficult. The fair way to raise the difficulty is to choose a character whose nearest neighbour is close: a supporting character from a large franchise, a villain who resembles a hero, a figure from a crowded period of history. Those picks are hard because of the data, not because of a trick, and the round still rewards careful answering.

A last check before you start

Ask yourself three things: do you know this character well enough to answer twenty questions without guessing, do you know which version you mean, and is the character famous enough that other people would describe them the way you would? Three yeses make a good round, and two make an interesting one. One makes a round that is likely to end in a wrong guess, which is still a valid way to spend two minutes.
