Thanos: A Character Guide for MindGenie AI

By Kent B Williams · Published November 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Thanos is the roster's most powerful villain, and he is stored as exactly that: fictional, superhuman, a villain, from another world, armed and part of a group. Most answers are clean, so the round closes fast once the villain question is answered honestly.

Role

Powers

Why the round is short

The roster holds few villains, and fewer who are also alien, superhuman, armed and organised. Each of those is a strong split, so the candidates collapse to a handful almost at once.

The stored partial is the fantasy question. Thanos is cosmic science fiction rather than fantasy, so the engine sits him between the two and keeps him alive beside Darth Vader for a question or two.

Who he is

A Titan who watched his world collapse, decided the cause was too many people, and spent decades gathering the stones to halve the universe. In the films he succeeds before the heroes undo it.

He is a villain written as the protagonist of his own story, which is why the engine also records leadership and a team.

Where players get tripped up

The human question. He looks humanoid, so yes feels right, but he is a Titan and the engine stores a no.

The costume question is a no as well. He wears armour, but the engine reserves that question for masks and disguises.

Why the Genie might guess Yoda instead

Closest match on the sheet: Yoda, at an average distance of 0.18 across 28 questions.

They differ on 7 questions: hero no against yes; villain yes against no; famous companion no against partly; mind over strength partly against yes; fantasy world partly against yes; school or academy no against partly; mentor figure no against yes. Answer those differences deliberately and the round resolves; answer by habit and the guess is a coin toss between the two.

The comic and the film want different things

In the comics, Thanos wipes out half the universe to impress Death, who is a character. The films replace that motive with resource scarcity and overpopulation, which is why film-only players describe him as a villain with an argument rather than a lover's errand.

The engine stores role, not motive, so both versions produce the same answers. The difference matters when you are deciding whether he counts as a leader.
