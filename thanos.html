Thanos: The Villain Whose Sheet Argues With Itself

By Kent B Williams · Updated November 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Thanos is the only character in the roster who is simultaneously a villain, a ruler, a weapons user, a science character and a wielder of cosmic power — and yet is not human, does not wear a mask, and is not the protagonist of his own films. Those contradictions are stored as four partial answers, and they are what make his rounds wobble.

A villain sheet with four partials

Eleven traits are firm yeses for Thanos: fictional, powered, from space, a villain, armed with a signature weapon, part of a group, known to almost everyone, associated with ruling, has a nemesis, speaks, and his real name is public. Thirteen are firm nos. The four partials are where the engine hedges, and each one is defensible:

  • Science and technology — partly. He is a genetic experiment in the comics and an engineer of cosmic weapons on screen, but he is not a scientist the way Tesla or GLaDOS is.
  • Animated — partly. He appears in animated series and games, though the character is defined by live action.
  • Mind over strength — partly. He is a strategist who out-plans the Avengers, and also a brawler who fights a planet's worth of heroes.
  • Fantasy world — partly. His story is science fiction with mythological furniture; the sheet will not call it fantasy outright.

Darth Vader is seven answers away

The closest character to Thanos in the whole roster is Darth Vader, at an average distance of 0.179 across the twenty-eight traits. They agree on twenty-one and split on exactly seven:

  • Human: Thanos no, Vader partly — a suit and a burned body complicate the answer.
  • Mask or costume: Thanos no, Vader yes.
  • Animated: Thanos partly, Vader no.
  • Transformation: Thanos no, Vader yes — Anakin becomes Vader.
  • Fantasy world: Thanos partly, Vader yes.
  • Paying job: Thanos no, Vader partly — an admiralty is not a job, but it functions like one.
  • Real name public: Thanos yes, Vader's identity is the secret.

Four of those seven are the difference between a masked enforcer and an unmasked warlord. If you answer "mask" for Thanos because of the helmet in Endgame, or "transformation" because he changes between films, you are answering for Vader and the round drifts.

Yoda sits at the same distance, for opposite reasons

Yoda is also 0.179 away, which is a neat illustration of how the scoring works: two characters can be equidistant from a third without resembling each other at all. Yoda and Thanos split on hero (Yoda yes, Thanos no), villain (no against yes), famous sidekick, mind over strength, fantasy world, school attendance and mentor status.

The engine does not care that one is a small green teacher and the other a purple warlord. It cares that if you answer "hero" and "mentor" you have described Yoda, and if you answer "villain" and "rules" you have described Thanos. Two answers separate them cleanly; everything else is noise.

GLaDOS, and the weapon question nobody expects

The third neighbour is GLaDOS at 0.196, and the split is instructive because it inverts the usual pattern. GLaDOS is not armed, is not from space, is not part of a team, is known only partly, and is the mind-over-strength character in the purest sense. Thanos is the reverse on every one of those.

So if a round has reached "is your character a villain?" and "are they known more for their mind than their strength?", the answer to the second question does most of the work. Yes points at GLaDOS and the science villains; partly points back at Thanos, who thinks and hits in roughly equal measure.

Why the snap is not a transformation

Players routinely answer yes to "does your character undergo a major transformation?" because the character's story contains the most famous change of state in modern film. The sheet says no, and it is right in the sense that matters here: the trait tracks a change of the character, not a change of the world. Anakin becomes Vader, Goku becomes a Super Saiyan, Maleficent becomes a dragon. Thanos wins and then retires to farm.

That distinction — character versus consequence — is one of the most common sources of wrong guesses across the whole roster, and it is worth remembering when you are thinking of any villain whose plan succeeds.

The human question, and why a Titan is a no

Thanos looks humanoid, speaks like a person and is played by an actor, so "human" feels like a yes. He is a Titan: in the comics a Titanian Eternal with a Deviant gene, on screen an alien from a destroyed world. The sheet stores no, which is the same answer Shrek, Yoda and SpongeBob carry.

This single answer is worth more than it looks. Saying yes to human while also saying villain, powers and space leaves almost no candidate — the engine will start guessing Iron Man or Thor and then lose confidence. Saying no keeps the Titan, the Jedi and the ogre in contention, which is where they belong.

Which Thanos is being described

The comics Thanos is a philosopher of death, courting Mistress Death and wiping out half of life as a gesture of devotion. The film Thanos is a resource economist with a farming hobby and a habit of explaining himself. The sheet follows the films, which is why "romance or marriage" is a no rather than a partial.

If you are thinking of the comic version — or of the animated appearances, which lean toward the comics — answer for the 2018 film character anyway. The traits are the same in every respect that the engine measures, except the motive, and motive is not a question it asks.

Playing Thanos when you want a long round

  1. Answer no to human, no to mask, no to transformation. Those three keep him apart from Vader.
  2. Answer yes to villain, yes to ruling, no to hero. Those keep him apart from Yoda.
  3. Answer partly to mind over strength. It is the only answer that separates him from GLaDOS cleanly.
  4. Answer no to the paying-job question. He is a warlord, not an employee.
  5. Expect the counter to reach fourteen. Thanos is a medium pick because the villain-plus-space-plus-ruler combination is rare, not because his round is short.

Length alone tells you nothing: with consistent answers every character in the roster runs to the fourteen-question cap. What does tell you something is a wrong final guess, and for Thanos that almost always traces back to a yes on mask or transformation — the two answers that pull Darth Vader forward.
