How to Get Better at Puzzle Games: The Habits That Actually Transfer

By Kent B Williams · Published November 27, 2026 · 9 min read

Almost everyone who plays a puzzle game regularly gets faster at it. That improvement is mostly recognition: the same grid, the same clue shapes, the same opening. The interesting question is what carries over from one game to the next. After a few thousand rounds across the games on this site — Sudoku, word guessing, memory tests, sorting and arithmetic sprints — a short list of habits shows up again and again, and none of them is a trick.

Habit one: scan by symbol, not by square

The most common beginner mistake in Sudoku is reading the grid cell by cell and asking “what can go here?”. That question is expensive: every empty cell has up to nine candidates, and checking each one means scanning a row, a column and a box. The players who finish a nine-by-nine grid in ten minutes ask the opposite question: “where can this digit go?”

Pick the digit that already appears most often. Its remaining homes are few, and each one is quickly ruled in or out by what is already on the board. One pass over the digits often places three or four of them, which changes the board enough that the next pass places more. The same habit works in Sudoku Mini and Sudoku Six, where a single pass frequently finishes the grid outright.

Habit two: work backwards from the constraint

Forwards thinking asks what you can do next. Backwards thinking asks what the last move had to be, and it is usually cheaper. In Equation Forge, where four numbers have to be connected by three operators to reach a target, the productive question is not “which operator shall I try first?” but “what does the final operation have to have been?”. A target only slightly above the largest number usually means a sum at the end; a target far above every number usually means multiplication.

The same principle explains why experienced crossword solvers read the enumeration before the clue. A seven-letter answer ending in -ING has a tiny candidate set, and knowing that before you know the definition is not cheating — it is using the constraint you were given. In Clue Anagrams the letters are a constraint of exactly that kind: count them, look at the ending, and the clue only has to confirm what the shape already suggests.

Habit three: chunk, do not list

Memory games punish lists. A list of eight digits is eight things to hold; the same eight digits as four pairs is four things. This is not a metaphor: short-term memory behaves like a fixed number of slots whose size is elastic, so the way to get more in is to make each item bigger. Phone numbers were seven digits long for decades for precisely this reason.

In Digit Echo, players who group the digits as they flash routinely reach nine or ten; players who repeat them one at a time stall around seven. In Grid Flash the chunking is spatial: “two in the top row, one in the bottom corner” is one phrase rather than three coordinates. In Order Recall it is narrative — the star before the cross, the cross before the circle — which turns a sequence into a sentence and survives the shuffle.

Habit four: know what a mistake costs before you make it

Every game has a different price for being wrong, and the best players calibrate their risk to it. In Shift Sort a swap is free to try and cheap to undo, so experimenting is rational. In Daily Clues each clue allows exactly one attempt, so a considered guess beats a fast one. In Fraction Ladder a wrong click costs nothing but a shake and a mistake on the scoreboard, which means the correct strategy is to place the value you are surest of rather than the one you can reach fastest.

This sounds obvious and is routinely ignored. Players guess in games where guessing is punished and deliberate in games where it is free, and the pattern is usually habit rather than judgement. Before the first move, ask what being wrong actually costs.

Habit five: use the clock as information

Timed games are not only harder; they tell you something. If a two-minute clue run leaves you with four solved clues and six skipped, the limiting factor is recognition speed, not knowledge, and the fix is a different one than more practice at the same pace. If you finish with eight solved and two wrong, the limit is accuracy. If you never reach the last clues, the limit is the clock.

That diagnosis is worth more than another ten rounds. Quick Sums and Missing Number both report a score and a streak separately for exactly this reason: a high score with a broken streak means speed, and a low score with a long streak means caution. The two need opposite corrections.

What does not transfer

Specific knowledge mostly stays where it was learned. Knowing the two-letter words in one crossword vocabulary does not help with a different one, and a memorised Sudoku opening does not help with a grid that starts elsewhere. What transfers is the shape of the search: look for constraints, work from the tightest one, and only guess when the price is low.

That is also why playing many different games is better practice than grinding one. Each game gives you a constraint you cannot dodge: a clock, a single attempt, a hidden pattern, an exact division. Switching between them forces you to re-read the constraint instead of running a routine, and re-reading constraints is the actual skill. The full games library exists partly for that reason, and the ten categories are a decent tour of the different kinds of constraint you can be given.

A short routine that fits in ten minutes

If you want to see improvement rather than just play, this is the sequence I would use. One Daily Sudoku for constraint scanning. One round of Daily Sequence for rule-hunting, which is backwards thinking in its purest form. One Sort Bins run for speed under a rule that changes, and one memory game — whichever you are worst at. Ten minutes, four different constraints, and a record kept in the browser so the comparison next week is honest.

The record matters more than the total. A puzzle game you play for a year without a scoreboard teaches you that you enjoy it; a puzzle game with a scoreboard teaches you where you are actually losing time. All of the games on this site keep their best score in localStorage and never send it anywhere, which makes that comparison private as well as free.

Put one of these habits to work

Start with the game whose constraint you find least comfortable. That is the one with something to teach you.
