Why Certain Fictional Characters Are Harder for AI to Guess

By Kent B Williams · Published November 27, 2026 · 9 min read

Players usually assume the hard characters are the obscure ones. In this roster they are almost never obscure: Batman is hard, and The Doctor is easy. Difficulty here is not a property of the character at all. It is a property of the space between the character and their nearest neighbour, and that space is produced by fiction's habit of reusing the same shapes.

How the roster decides

Every character carries twenty-eight trait values — 1.0 for yes, 0.0 for no, 0.5 for the genuine in-between. The distance between two characters is the mean absolute difference across all twenty-eight. A character is filed as hard when their nearest neighbour sits closer than 0.145, medium below 0.185, and easy above that.

That means a character's band is decided by one number: how near their closest lookalike is. Fame, screen time and how much you personally like them do not enter into it.

Two of those pairs are historical figures, three are animated, three are game or film fighters, and one is a pair of schoolchildren in the same book. That distribution is the whole argument of this article: overlap is not a genre problem, it is a trait problem.

The archetype effect, stated plainly

The twenty-eight questions are broad. They ask whether a character is fictional, human, powered, masked, armed, royal, famous, animated, academic, romantic, aquatic. Those categories describe archetypes, not individuals. Fiction is built out of archetypes, so any two characters drawn from the same archetype will answer the same way to almost every question — and the engine, which only sees answers, cannot tell them apart.

This is why the list of hard characters reads like a list of well-known names rather than a list of obscurities. The engine is not failing to know who Batman is. It is failing to find a question whose answer separates Batman from Iron Man.

Case study: Lincoln and Cleopatra at 0.089

The closest pair in the entire roster is a nineteenth-century American president and a first-century Egyptian queen, which sounds absurd until you list what the questions actually ask. Both are real. Both are human. Both are associated with ruling. Both are known to almost everyone. Both speak. Both have public names. Both are the subject of a story that is not a fantasy, not animated, not romantic in the stored sense.

What separates them is thin: a weapon for one, a sea voyage and a romance for the other, and the answers to questions about nemeses and transformation. Lincoln's nearest-neighbour distance of 0.089 is what puts him in the hard band on the history page, and it is entirely deserved.

Case study: Furiosa and Joan of Arc at 0.107

A fictional desert raider and a fifteenth-century French commander sit 0.107 apart because the sheet sees both as human, unpowered, armed, mission-driven and famous within their own story. One is animated-adjacent cinema and the other is history, and the sheet does capture that — but the fiction answer and the era questions are only worth so much when eight other traits are identical.

This is the pair most likely to produce a wrong guess that feels insulting to the player. Nothing about the two characters is similar in tone; everything about their answers is similar in structure.

Case study: Goku and Pikachu at 0.125

The anime pool is the smallest in the roster, and these two anchor it. Both are fictional, powered, from an animated series, not meaningfully human in the sheet's sense, and widely known. They split on weapons, sidekicks, royalty, protagonists and speech — five answers, all of which arrive late because the early questions have already confirmed the archetype.

If you are thinking of Pikachu and the engine asks about a signature weapon, you are being handed the round. Answer "no" and the split opens; answer "yes" out of a vague memory of Thunderbolt and the round closes on Goku.

Why half-answers make it worse

A trait value of 0.5 is the engine's way of saying "this character could go either way on this question". It is honest, and it is also the single biggest compressor of distance in the data. A character with four half-values can only ever be 0.5 away from a player who answers decisively, and 0.0 away from a player who uses "not sure" — which is why the middle answers change which characters look close, not merely how close they are.

The article on ambiguous answers goes through that arithmetic. The short version: half-answers make characters interchangeable, and interchangeability is exactly what makes a round hard.

Hard is not the same as obscure

The clearest way to see this is to compare two characters from the same franchise. The Doctor is filed as easy and Batman as hard. One is a British science-fiction lead with a devoted but narrow audience; the other is among the most recognisable fictional characters alive. The Doctor's trait vector is strange — alien, partly human, powered, a famous companion, a nemesis, not the sole protagonist, no weapon — and strangeness is distance. Batman's vector is the most copied template in comics: human, unpowered, masked, armed with gadgets, team-adjacent, known to everyone.

So the practical advice for anyone picking a character to stump the engine is not "pick something obscure". It is "pick something whose answers are shared by two or three other roster entries".

What would actually fix it

  • Add distinguishing questions. A question about siblings, about whether the character has a secret identity, or about whether their story is told across more than one medium would split several of the pairs above immediately.
  • Tighten the half-values. Every 0.5 in the data is a character deliberately placed between two groups; using fewer of them would widen distances but also make more guesses wrong.
  • Accept the cap. Some pairs cannot be separated by twenty-eight broad questions, and the fourteen-question ceiling is the honest admission of that.

Playing the close pairs

  1. Identify the archetype you are in before answering — real ruler, armoured fighter, school wizard, animated non-human.
  2. Save the deciding answer for when it is asked, and answer it firmly. "Probably" on a separating question is how a round is lost.
  3. Expect fourteen questions. Close pairs never clear the margin; that is what "close" means.
  4. If the guess is wrong, the useful question is not "why didn't it know?" but "which answer would have separated them?" — and the guide for that character usually names it.

Try the algorithm on a character you know

The fastest way to understand a narrowing engine is to watch it narrow. Pick someone obvious, then someone close to them.
