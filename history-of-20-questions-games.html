Where It All Started

📜 The History of 20 Questions Games

MindGenie AI Team · Updated August 2026 · 6 min read

MindGenie AI feels like a modern piece of software — a browser game, an AI-driven guessing engine, instant results on your phone. But the game underneath it is close to a century old. Here's where the "think of something, answer yes or no, get identified" format actually came from.

A 19th-century parlor game

Long before radio or computers, "Twenty Questions" was already a well-known parlor game in the United States, played at gatherings throughout the 1800s. The rules were exactly what you'd expect: one player silently picked a person, place, or thing, and everyone else took turns asking yes-or-no questions, trying to identify it in twenty questions or fewer. It required no equipment and no setup — just a group of people and a bit of patience.

From parlor game to national radio hit

The format made an unusually smooth jump to broadcast media. On February 2, 1946, Twenty Questions premiered as a weekly radio quiz show on the Mutual Broadcasting System, built around the Van Deventer family — newsman Fred Van Deventer, his wife, and their two children — who served as the regular panel guessing subjects mailed in by listeners. One of the most frequently submitted subjects, according to accounts of the show, was Winston Churchill's cigar.

The show was a hit. It ran on radio for roughly eight years, moved to television in 1949, and continued in various forms on NBC, ABC, and the DuMont network into the mid-1950s. Its success also spread internationally — a British version launched on BBC radio in 1947, and other countries developed their own adaptations soon after.

The leap to computers

The format's structure — a sequence of binary (yes/no) questions narrowing down one answer out of many — turned out to be a natural fit for computing, decades before anyone built a guessing website. Programmers experimented with "20 Questions" style decision-tree programs as early demonstrations of how a machine could appear to reason through a problem, since the format maps almost directly onto a data structure computer scientists already used: the binary decision tree.

Akinator brings it to the browser

The most well-known modern version of the format arrived in 2007, when French developer Arnaud Mégret released a browser game originally called "Devinettor," built around the same idea his own family used to play. The game was quickly renamed Akinator and released by the company Elokence, and it spread fast — going viral internationally through 2008 and expanding to mobile apps by 2010. Instead of a human panel, Akinator used a purpose-built algorithm to select each question and update its guess after every answer, learning from the outcome of every game played by every user, not just one family.

That single change — swapping a fixed human panel for a constantly-updating algorithm — is what separates the 1946 radio format from the games built on it today. The rules are identical. The "panelist" is now a piece of software that's played millions of rounds and never forgets a single answer.

Why the format has lasted this long

Eighty years is a long time for any game format to survive largely unchanged, radio show to smartphone. The reason is simple: the core loop — think of something, get asked a short series of questions, feel a small jolt of surprise when the answer lands — doesn't need updating. Only the "panelist" asking the questions has changed, from a family of radio guests to a self-improving guessing engine that gets a little sharper with every round.
