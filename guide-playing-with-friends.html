Playing MindGenie AI With Friends, Family or a Class

By Kent B Williams · Published 27 November 2026 · About the writer

The game was built for one person with one character in mind, and that is how most rounds happen. But a surprising number of people open it in a room with other people around, and the moment a second person joins, a quiet solo deduction puzzle turns into something closer to a parlour game. That change is not a problem, as long as everyone agrees on three things before the first question: who is answering, whose character it is, and what counts as a fair answer.

This piece lays out those agreements. It also suggests a scoring system, a classroom format and a few variations, all of which are conveniences rather than rules the site enforces. Nothing in the game needs an account, a download or a sign-up, so passing a phone around or handing out laptops both work without setup.

Why a group round behaves differently

In a solo round, only one person knows what the answers should be, so there is nothing to disagree about. With a group, the character belongs to one player, but the questions arrive in front of everybody, and everyone has an opinion about the correct answer. A round that should take eight questions can stall at the second one while two people argue about whether a character counts as human.

The engine cannot resolve that argument, and it should not try. It reads one answer per question, and a second answer arriving halfway through sends the round somewhere neither player intended. The fix is procedural: one person answers, and their answer is final for that round.

Three ways to set it up

Pass one phone

The simplest option, and the one that keeps the whole group looking at the same screen. The player whose turn it is keeps their character to themselves, then reads each question aloud and taps the answer after the group has had its say. The phone stays with that player for the entire round, which avoids the confusion of passing it between questions.

One screen for the room

If you have a laptop or a television with a browser, put the game on the big screen and let the group answer out loud. This is the best format for a quiz night or a family evening, because everyone can see how many questions have been used and how the field is narrowing. It also makes the final guess feel like a shared moment rather than something one person saw first.

Everyone on their own device

Competitive groups often prefer this: each player runs their own round at the same time, with the same theme, and the room compares how many questions each character took. It needs no coordination and no turn order, and it gives you a genuine scoreboard at the end.

House rules that keep a round fair

  1. One version of the character. A character with a long history usually has several versions. If one player is thinking of a film and another of a comic, the answers will contradict each other for reasons that have nothing to do with the engine.
  2. The answerer's word is final. Even when the group disagrees, the person holding the character decides. Arguing after the fact is what turns a two-minute game into a ten-minute argument.
  3. No lying to make it harder. Choosing a difficult character is fair; answering falsely is not. A false answer makes the round random, and a random round is not more fun to watch.
  4. Soft answers are allowed. "Probably", "Probably Not" and "Not Sure" exist for exactly this situation. Using them is not cheating, and it is usually a better description of a group's shared knowledge than a confident yes.
  5. No character changes mid-round. If the character turns out to be unplayable, finish the round honestly and start again. Switching halfway is the one move that makes the result meaningless.

A simple scoring system

If your group wants a winner, the cleanest measure is how long the Genie took. Award three points to a player whose character was guessed in ten questions or fewer, two points for eleven to fourteen, and one point for a character the Genie failed to find. A wrong guess is worth two points to the player, because stumping the Genie is the harder achievement. Play five rounds and add the totals. Groups that prefer co-operation can ignore scoring entirely and simply try to beat their own best round.

Answering as a committee

When a group shares one character, appoint a single answerer before the first question. Everyone else may discuss, but only the answerer taps. Disagreement is useful information: if half the table thinks a character counts as human and half does not, "Probably" is a more honest answer than either extreme, and it keeps the round alive.

Two habits make committee rounds work. First, let the answerer repeat the question before answering, so nobody is answering a question they misheard. Second, keep speculation short. A round is capped at fourteen questions, and a group that debates each one for a minute will spend a quarter of an hour on a game designed to take two.

Playing in a classroom

Teachers use guessing games for reasons that have nothing to do with the Genie: they get a whole class talking about categories, and they make the vocabulary of description concrete. A ten-minute block works well. Put students in pairs, give each pair one device, and ask one student to think of a character while the other answers. Then swap.

The useful part comes afterwards. Ask pairs to name the question that narrowed the field the most, and the one that wasted a turn. That turns a game into a short lesson on how questions carry information, which is the same idea behind the site's explanation of how the engine chooses what to ask. The themes make it easy to tie the activity to a unit: films for media, history for a period you are studying, anime or games for students who would otherwise switch off.

When the Genie gets it wrong

It will happen, especially with a group, because groups pick characters that are famous to them and obscure to everyone else. Mark the guess as wrong, let the round end, and start a new one rather than trying to steer the old one. Every rating is recorded as an anonymous statistic, which is how the roster and the questions get improved over time, so a wrong guess is a small contribution rather than a failure.

Variations worth trying

  • Theme night. Restrict everyone to one theme, such as films or history, so the group's knowledge is tested evenly.
  • Fewest questions wins. Each player picks the most distinctive character they can think of and the shortest round takes the prize.
  • Stump the table. Everyone picks from the list of characters that give the Genie the most trouble, and the group tries to keep the round alive as long as possible.
  • Chain rounds. The player who was guessed correctly chooses the next character, which keeps a large group moving without a turn order to remember.

Keeping it fun for younger players

Children usually enjoy the game most when they pick characters they know well, and least when an adult picks something from forty years ago. Let them choose, and resist the urge to correct their answers: a child who says a cartoon character has powers because the character is strong is answering the question as they understand it, and the round still works. If the Genie guesses wrong, that is the interesting part, not a defeat.

The game is free, needs no account, and runs in a browser on a phone, which makes it easy to hand over without signing anything up or installing anything. That is also why it works well as a five-minute filler while waiting for food, a bus or the rest of a class.
