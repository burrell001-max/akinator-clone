Probably, Probably Not, Not Sure: What the Middle Answers Do

By the MindGenie AI team · Published 27 November 2026 · About the writer

Most guessing games give you two buttons. This one gives you five, and the three in the middle are the reason a round can end well even when you are not certain about the character you picked. They are also the reason a round can end badly, if they are used as a way of avoiding a decision.

Here is what each option is actually for, and how to choose between them without turning the round into guesswork.

Why a third option is needed at all

A character is not a tidy set of facts. Ask ten people whether a morally complicated anti-hero counts as a hero and you will get a split. Ask whether a character has powers when their abilities come from a suit of armour and the answer depends on which word you emphasise. Forcing those questions into yes or no does not make the answer more accurate; it makes it confidently wrong.

A soft answer lets you say what you actually know: that the character leans one way but not decisively. The engine treats it as weaker evidence than a confident answer, which is exactly right. It narrows the field a little rather than a lot, and it does not rule out characters who would have been eliminated by a hard answer.

What each middle answer is telling the engine

Probably means the trait is more true than false for the character you have in mind. Use it when you would defend a yes if someone pushed you, but you would not stake much on it.

Probably not is the mirror image: more false than true, with room for doubt. It is the right answer for a character who mostly lacks a trait but has one version or one scene where they do not.

Not sure is not a vote. It carries no useful signal in either direction, and the engine will move on as though the question had not been asked. It exists so you are never forced to invent an answer.

The cost of a confident wrong answer

A hard yes or no is the most useful thing you can give the engine when it is right, and the most damaging when it is wrong. Suppose you are thinking of a character with magic, and a question asks whether your character has powers. If you answer yes with confidence, every character without powers is pushed far down the list in one step. If that answer was wrong, the character you actually meant may now be almost unreachable, and the round will spend its remaining questions trying to recover.

A soft answer costs you a question or two of progress. A confident answer that contradicts the rest of your answers costs you the round. When the two options are weighed against each other, the softer answer is usually the cheaper mistake.

Two examples worth remembering

Take the powers question with a character in powered armour. The person inside has no abilities of their own, but the equipment does things no human can. Answering yes because the suit is powerful pulls the round towards characters with innate powers; answering no and then answering yes to technology keeps the round on the person rather than the machine. Neither answer is wrong in isolation. The one you choose has to match the other answers you are giving.

Take the romance question with an animated queen whose story is about her sister rather than a prince. Answering yes because the character is royalty and the film is a fairy tale sends the round towards a different kind of lead. Answering no is more accurate, and it stays accurate as long as you do not later answer that the story is a romance.

When to use Not sure

Not sure is best saved for details the story genuinely does not settle: a character's exact age, whether a specific relationship counts as family, whether an adaptation's version of an event is canon. It is a poor answer for things you simply have not thought about, because the questions are usually asking about something that is reasonably well known. If you can make a judgement, make a soft one rather than abstaining.

A practical rule

Answer as though a friend were asking you the same question in conversation. If you would say "yes, definitely", answer yes. If you would say "yes, I think so", answer probably. If you would say "I have no idea", answer not sure. Rounds that follow that rule tend to end on a character that at least resembles the one you had in mind, which is a better outcome than a clean, confident, wrong guess.

Keep practising

The quickest way to feel the difference is to play the same character twice, once with confident answers and once with honest soft ones, and compare how many questions each round takes.
