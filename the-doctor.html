The Doctor: Eleven Yeses, Five Half-Answers and a Name Nobody Knows

By Kent B Williams · Updated November 27, 2026 · 7 min read

The Doctor is the strangest entry in the TV pool and the easiest one to guess, which sounds like a contradiction until you look at why. His sheet is not close to anyone else's: eleven firm yeses, five deliberate halves and twelve nos, with a science answer, an alien answer and a secret-name answer that almost nobody else in the roster shares. The difficulty page files him as easy for a reason — his nearest neighbour is 0.214 away, one of the four widest gaps in the whole roster, joint with Mulan, Roy Batty and Spider-Man.

Quick facts

First appearance
An Unearthly Child, BBC Television, 1963

Played by

Role
Time Lord traveller, TARDIS pilot, occasional universe-fixer
Signature item
The sonic screwdriver — a tool, not a weapon, which matters below

What the twenty-eight answers say about a Time Lord

Eleven traits are firm yeses: fictional, powered, a hero, associated with science and genius-level intelligence, from outer space, has a famous companion, has a nemesis, known more for mind than muscle, undergoes a major transformation, speaks, and is the sole title-billed protagonist. Five are halves: human, part of a team, known to almost everyone on Earth, story set in a fantasy world, and ancient mentor. Twelve are nos.

Three of those nos are the ones players argue with:

  • Weapon. The sonic screwdriver opens doors, scans walls and occasionally rewires a console. It has never been stored as a signature weapon, and that single no separates him from a dozen armed leads.
  • Royalty. He is a renegade Time Lord, not a ruler. Answering yes because Gallifrey had a High Council pushes the round toward Thor.
  • Public name. This is the interesting one. The sheet stores the Doctor's true name as not publicly known — because it isn't, on screen or off. Answering "well, he's called the Doctor" flips a trait that is doing real work.

Goku at 0.214: the nearest neighbour, and still far

The closest character to the Doctor in the whole roster is Goku, across pools, at an average distance of 0.214. They agree on twenty-one of twenty-eight traits — both alien, both powered, both heroes, both transformed by their story, both sole protagonists — and split on seven, every one of which is a category rather than a detail:

  • Science and mind: the Doctor yes, Goku no, on both.
  • Animation: Goku yes, the Doctor no.
  • Fame: Goku a firm yes, the Doctor a half.
  • Mentor: the Doctor a half, Goku no.
  • Public name: Goku yes, the Doctor no.
  • Sole protagonist: the Doctor yes, Goku no.

Read that list as a set of questions and you have the round. Each is a question a player can answer confidently, which is why this pairing resolves instead of grinding: there is no ambiguity to hide in.

Cortana and Eleven at 0.232: the powered outsider shape

Two neighbours tie for second. Cortana shares nineteen of twenty-eight answers with the Doctor; Eleven shares eighteen. Both pairings split on the same core: the Doctor is an alien where Cortana is software and Eleven is human, both companions and nemeses are firmer for the Doctor, and the Doctor is the sole protagonist while neither of the others is.

The Eleven pairing is the more instructive one, because it splits on ten traits — the largest divergence in the group — and still lands at 0.232. That is what a small pool does: with only nine TV characters in the roster, even a ten-question disagreement moves the average by about a fifth of a point. The pool size, not the writing, is why the TV characters cluster.

Regeneration counts as transformation

The sheet marks the transformation trait as a firm yes, and it is not being generous. A character whose lead actor changes because the character's body changes is the cleanest possible example of a transformation story: same person, new face, new temperament, new era. If you are thinking of a specific Doctor and answer "no, he doesn't transform", you are answering for one incarnation rather than the character, and the round will drift toward Eleven, who also transforms but is not an alien.

The same logic explains the human half. The Doctor looks human and is not; the sheet records that tension as 0.5 rather than pretending it away. Answer "yes, he looks human" firmly and you have added 0.5 to the true character's distance on a question where a half answer would have cost nothing.

Which Doctor is being described

The character has been played by more than a dozen lead actors since 1963, and the versions are not interchangeable. The first Doctor is an irritable fugitive who kidnaps two schoolteachers; the fourth is a scarf-wearing bohemian; the seventh is a manipulator; the ninth is a war survivor; the eleventh is a fairy-tale figure; the thirteenth is a tinkerer; the fifteenth is the most openly emotional of the modern run. The 1996 television film, the Big Finish audio plays and the animated reconstructions all add further versions.

None of that changes the trait sheet, because none of it touches the questions being asked. What it changes is how a player answers them. A fan thinking of the seventh Doctor may answer "manipulative, not heroic" and lose the hero trait; a fan thinking of the fourth may answer "not associated with science" because he is comic rather than technical. The sheet describes the character across the run: hero, science, alien, transforming, talkative, sole protagonist. Answer at that level and the round behaves.

Where players lose this round

  • Treating the sonic screwdriver as a weapon. It is the single most common mis-answer for this character, and it converts a clean split into a fight with Thor and Kratos.
  • Answering "yes" to a public name. The Doctor's name is the one piece of information the character has spent sixty years refusing to give. The sheet respects that.
  • Answering "no" to fame. He is not a household name the way Batman is, but he is recognised well beyond his own fandom, which is why the trait is stored as a half rather than a no.
  • Answering "no" to companion. The companion is the format. Any round that denies it pushes toward Cortana, who has no companion at all.
  • Answering "mentor" with a firm no. He spends a great deal of the modern run explaining things to a younger traveller. The sheet calls that a half, not a no.

Playing a Time Lord round on purpose

  1. Say yes to science and yes to alien. Nothing else in the roster combines them as cleanly, and those two answers do most of the narrowing.
  2. Say no to the weapon. It is the answer that keeps the round away from the armed leads.
  3. Say no to the public name. It is the trait the character is famous for withholding.
  4. Use the halves for human, fame, team and mentor. A half answer here costs the true character nothing and keeps the alternatives honest.
  5. Do not read the question count as a signal. Every character in the roster runs to the fourteen-question cap with consistent answers, including this one.
