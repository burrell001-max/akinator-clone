How to Read a Character Guide on This Site

By Kent B Williams · Published 27 November 2026 · About the writer

Every character with a page on this site has a guide, and the guides all share the same shape. That is deliberate. Once you have read one, you can read any of them, and you can find the specific thing you came for in about twenty seconds. This piece explains what each block is for, why the blocks are in that order, and what the guides are deliberately not.

The short version: a guide here is not a wiki entry. It is a description of how the engine behaves with one character, written so that a player can answer confidently and understand a wrong guess instead of being confused by it.

The quick facts block

The first thing on the page is a short table: first appearance, creators, real identity if there is one, home city, powers, and the media the character appears in. Taken together, those fields cover most of the broad questions the engine asks. First appearance gives an era. Creators and home city hint at medium and genre. Powers answers the single most useful dividing question in the whole roster. Media tells you which theme the character is likely filed under.

It is not there as trivia. If you are halfway through a round and the Genie asks whether your character first appeared before 1980, the table is the fastest way to settle the answer without leaving the page.

Who is this character

A short prose section follows, describing the character in plain terms. Its job is recognition rather than biography: enough to confirm that the character you are thinking of is the one on the page, and enough to notice when a character has several versions. If you find yourself thinking "well, in the second film it was different", that reaction is the point. It is a warning about the next section.

Traits that define them

This is the answer key. Each guide lists the likely trait answers for that character: fictional or real, human or not, costumed or not, powered or not, wealthy or not, and so on, with a line of explanation where the obvious answer is wrong. Batman's guide, for instance, separates "has powers" from "is exceptionally skilled", because those are different questions and players conflate them constantly.

Read this section before you play, not during. Trying to consult a page while answering questions is the fastest way to lose track of which version you committed to.

Why the Genie finds them quickly, or does not

Each guide explains the character's position in the roster: which combination of traits makes them distinctive, and which other characters share enough of those traits to cause hesitation. This is where the useful idea of a nearest neighbour shows up in practice. A character whose closest match is far away is guessed in a handful of questions. A character whose closest match differs by one trait takes most of a round, and sometimes more than the engine has.

That section is also the honest answer to "why did it take fourteen questions". It is rarely about how famous the character is. It is about how crowded their corner of the trait space happens to be.

Where players get tripped up

The most practical section on the page, and the one most worth reading twice. It covers three recurring problems:

  • Versions. Long-running characters change between films, seasons and reboots. The guide names the questions where versions diverge, so you know where a soft answer is safer than a confident one.
  • Wording. Some questions sound like one thing and mean another. "Special abilities" is the classic: a highly trained human has abilities, but not powers.
  • Lookalikes. The guide lists the characters the engine most often confuses with this one, and the trait that separates them. That list doubles as a set of alternative picks if you want a harder round.

When to pick them, and answers that go wrong

Two shorter sections follow the main explanation. The first says what kind of round the character produces, which is useful when you are choosing a pick for a group or for someone new to the game. The second names the specific wrong answer that most often derails a round, which is usually more actionable than general advice.

The FAQ and the related cards

Each guide ends with four questions that players actually ask, answered briefly, followed by cards linking to neighbouring characters and to the category page. The category pages group their characters by how hard they are to guess, so the cards are a route from an easy pick to a harder one in the same theme. Following two or three of them is a better way to find an interesting character than picking at random.

What the guides deliberately leave out

There are no win rates, no question averages, no rankings and no invented quotes. The site does not publish statistics about individual characters, so a guide that offered them would be making them up. What the guides do contain is a description of the machinery as it applies to one character, and a set of trait answers you can check for yourself by playing.

They also avoid story summaries. If you want the plot of a film, a guide is the wrong place to look, and padding one with a synopsis would make the useful parts harder to find.

Using a guide in under a minute

  1. Skim the quick facts to confirm you mean the right character.
  2. Read the trait list, and note any question where you would hesitate.
  3. Check the lookalikes, so a mid-round suggestion does not throw you.
  4. Decide on one version, then start a round and answer for that version only.

If the Genie guesses wrong anyway, come back to the "answers that go wrong" section. Nine times out of ten the cause is in the first line of it.
