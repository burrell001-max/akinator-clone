Slide Fifteen

A sliding tile puzzle: put the numbers back in order using the one empty square.

Click a tile beside the gap, or use the arrow keys. Arrows move a tile into the gap.

How to play

A square is empty. Click any tile that shares an edge with it and the tile slides across. Your job is to slide the numbered tiles until they run 1, 2, 3 upwards from the top-left, with the gap in the bottom-right corner. On the four-by-four board that means 1 to 15 in reading order, with the gap last.

The arrow keys work too: left and right move the tile that sits beside the gap on that side, up and down likewise. Moves are counted only when a tile actually slides.

Rules and scoring

  • Only tiles sharing an edge with the gap can move; diagonal neighbours cannot.
  • Every shuffle is produced by making a few hundred legal moves from the solved board, so a solution always exists — no unsolvable boards.
  • Your score is the number of moves, with time as a tiebreaker for your own records.
  • The best move count for each board size is kept in this browser under mg-slide-best.
  • Shuffling starts a fresh board, resets the counters and keeps the best score.

Solving it without guessing

Do not try to place all sixteen tiles at once. Solve the top row first and then never touch it again: park 1 and 2 in the top-left, and slot 3 into the corner by using the second row as a workspace. Once the top row is locked, do the same for the left column, then for the remaining three-by-three block, then the two-by-two one. Each stage is smaller than the last, which is why a fifteen puzzle falls apart into five easy puzzles instead of one hard one.

The last two-by-two block is the one that catches people. If the final three tiles are in a cycle that will not resolve, you have almost certainly broken an earlier row by sliding into it — go back and check that the top row and left column are still clean. A board that has been shuffled legally always has a solution; a stuck final block usually means a move you thought was free moved a settled tile.

Why this puzzle still works

The fifteen puzzle was a Victorian craze, and the reason it lasted is that the constraint is perfect: exactly one gap, so every move costs something and every position is reachable in one of two parity classes. Because the shuffle here is always legal, you never meet the famously impossible half — and because the goal is ordered, you can always see how far you have come.
