How Does an AI Guessing Game Work?

MindGenie AI Team · Updated August 2026 · 6 min read

Every round of MindGenie AI looks like magic: you think of a character, answer a handful of questions, and the Genie names the exact person you had in mind. There's no magic involved — just a well-understood piece of computer science that's been narrowing down mystery answers since long before computers existed.

It starts with an old parlor game

The format the Genie uses — think of something, answer yes-or-no questions, get identified within a limited number of tries — comes from the classic guessing game "20 Questions." One player picks a subject, and everyone else tries to identify it using only yes-or-no questions, ideally in twenty or fewer. Every AI guessing game, MindGenie AI included, is really just a digital version of that idea, run against a huge database of characters instead of one human opponent.

The core trick: splitting the crowd in half

Imagine the Genie starts with a pool of thousands of possible characters. A badly chosen first question — "Are you thinking of a wizard?" — only rules out a small slice of that pool. A well-chosen question splits the remaining candidates roughly down the middle, no matter what you're thinking of.

That's the same principle behind binary search, the technique used to look something up quickly in a huge sorted list. Each answer you give doesn't just remove a few characters — it removes roughly half of everyone still standing. Ask enough well-placed questions like that, and even a pool of 100,000 characters collapses to a single name in well under twenty rounds. Mathematically, halving a pool of size N takes only about log₂(N) questions — for 100,000 characters, that's around 17.

Choosing the "best" next question

The hard part isn't the halving math, it's figuring out which question actually splits the current pool evenly. Behind the scenes, the engine tracks how previous players answered each question for each character, and uses that history to estimate, for the group of characters still in play, which question is closest to a 50/50 split right now. This is closely related to a concept from information theory called entropy — roughly, how much a question is expected to reduce our uncertainty. The engine favors high-entropy questions early on and switches to narrower, more specific ones as the pool shrinks.

This is also why the Genie's questions can feel oddly specific later in a round — "Does this character often carry a weapon?" makes little sense as a first question, but it's exactly the kind of question that separates the last two or three characters still in the running.

Learning from every round

A static guessing tree would get stale fast — new characters become popular, old ones fade, and a fixed set of questions eventually stops working well. Instead, systems like this treat every completed game as a small piece of training data. When you rate whether the Genie's final guess was right or wrong, that feedback nudges the weighting of the question-and-character associations involved in your round, ever so slightly. Multiply that by thousands of games, and the question-selection engine keeps adapting on its own — no manual reprogramming required.

Handling "probably," "not sure," and getting it wrong

Real answers aren't always a clean yes or no, which is why the Genie offers "Probably," "Probably not," and "Not sure" as well. Internally, each of those maps to a confidence value between 0 and 1 rather than a hard true/false, so a hesitant answer nudges the probability of each remaining character instead of eliminating or confirming them outright. That's also why an occasional wrong guess doesn't mean the system is broken — a string of uncertain answers, or a genuinely obscure character, can leave two or three candidates in a near tie right up to the final question.

Why this approach scales so well

  • It's category-agnostic. The same halving logic works whether you're thinking of a movie villain, an anime protagonist, or a historical figure — the engine doesn't need separate rules for each category.
  • It gets smarter with use. More players means more signal on which questions actually distinguish similar characters.
  • It fails gracefully. Even when the guess is wrong, the near-misses are usually plausible — a mix-up between two characters who share a lot of traits, not a random miss.

So the next time the Genie nails a character you were sure would stump it, you'll know there's no actual mind-reading going on — just a few thousand well-placed yes-or-no questions, refined by everyone who's played before you.
